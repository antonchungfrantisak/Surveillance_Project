Introduction

With more than 120,000 cameras across more than 6,000 communities that
record 20 billion license-plate scans per month, \emph{Flock Safety}, or
Flock, had marketed its system as an ideal tool for fighting crime like
finding a stolen car carrying a 14-month-old child (Flock Safety, n.d.;
Harwell et al., n.d.). But by using automated license plate readers
(ALPRs) like Flock's massive camera network, at least 50 law-enforcement
officers were able to track their subjects' daily lives, such as to
stalk women without their knowledge or consent (Harwell et al., n.d.).
Such a polarizing tool---both used for very good and very bad---has
faced overwhelming recent scrutiny from the populace, media, and
politicians because it enables law enforcement to monitor ordinary
people. This essay argues that the (1) media theory of fear, (2)
political elite theory of fear, and (3) political culture theory of fear
may help explain why fear of surveillance of ordinary people for law
enforcement appears to have become more prominent among some Americans.

This essay first introduces how interest in Flock has increased using
Google Search data. It then explores polling data on Flock and ALPRs,
the amount of news articles on Flock, and examples of political elites
across the U.S. and political spectrum taking negative stances toward
Flock. Last, it proposes three explanations for why this fear has become
more prominent: (1) the media theory of fear; (2) the political elite
theory of fear; and (3) the political culture theory of fear.

\section{The Fear of Surveillance of Ordinary People for Law
Enforcement}\label{the-fear-of-surveillance-of-ordinary-people-for-law-enforcement}

Fear about surveillance of ordinary people for law enforcement gained
renewed attention in Summer 2026 amid the debate over Flock Safety
cameras (See Figure 1), but such concerns had already been expressed as
early as 2012 by the ACLU (American Civil Liberties Union 2012). The
general concern is that law enforcement has misused and may continue to
misuse surveillance of ordinary people for personal reasons or to
monitor their lawful activities without a legitimate reason (Harwell et
al., n.d.). As of late, (1) public opinion toward Flock has grown more
negative, (2) news coverage about Flock has increased, and (3) political
elites have criticized Flock.

\subsection{Opinion Polls about Flock and
ALPRs}\label{opinion-polls-about-flock-and-alprs}

\pandocbounded{\includegraphics[keepaspectratio]{CMOS_18_UNIVERSAL_QUARTO_TEMPLATE_files/figure-pdf/unnamed-chunk-3-1.png}}

\pandocbounded{\includegraphics[keepaspectratio]{CMOS_18_UNIVERSAL_QUARTO_TEMPLATE_files/figure-pdf/unnamed-chunk-3-2.png}}

The first piece of evidence is that negative public opinion toward Flock
has increased recently (See Figure 2). Polling data shows that the
percentage of surveyed Americans who viewed Flock unfavorably rose from
41 to 52 percent between late August and late September (Springs 2026).
These descriptive data show that views of Flock cameras became more
negative at the same time that search interest in Flock increased (See
Figures 1--2). This pattern thus warrants a further investigation of the
broader category to which Flock cameras belong---namely, ALPRs for law
enforcement.

The descriptive data on Americans' views of law enforcement's use of
ALPRs like Flock cameras mirror the same shift in the negative direction
observed in public opinion toward Flock (See Figure 3). Across two polls
that asked an identical question in 2025 and 2026, opposition to law
enforcement using ALPRs rose from 33 to 46 percent, whereas support for
it fell from 45 to 38 percent (YouGov 2026, 2025). These data expand the
descriptive trend established previously to law enforcement using ALPRs
in general. Therefore, it seems that public opinion toward ALPRs became
more negative in the second survey, in whose time frame Flock also
became an important issue. Although these data cannot establish a causal
link between Flock's salience and increasing opposition toward law
enforcement's use of ALPRs, they indicate that the shift in opinion was
not limited to Flock.

\subsection{Mentions of Flock in the
Media}\label{mentions-of-flock-in-the-media}

\pandocbounded{\includegraphics[keepaspectratio]{CMOS_18_UNIVERSAL_QUARTO_TEMPLATE_files/figure-pdf/unnamed-chunk-4-1.png}}

The second piece of evidence is that the amount of news coverage that
mentions Flock has increased (See Figure 4). Starting during July 2026
in the \emph{Nexis Uni} database, similar to the Google Search metrics,
news articles began to mention Flock much more frequently, rising from
under 500 articles per month from January through June to over 1,000 in
July, over 3,000 in August, and around 4,000 articles from September 1
through September 27, 2026 (NexusUni 2026). Thus, the increase in news
articles that mentioned Flock occurred in a similar time frame to the
rise in unfavorable views of Flock and opposition to law enforcement's
use of ALPRs (See Figures 2--4).

\subsection{Flock in Politics}\label{flock-in-politics}

The last piece of evidence involves political elites. Texas State
Senator Lois Kolkhorst (R) released an op-ed in August, in which she
argued that ``the use of Flock cameras aims to be a frightening new web
of warrantless mass surveillance\ldots{} One could reasonably think that
we are only a pandemic away from ushering in a `social credit score'
similar to the Chinese government, which can limit its citizens'
movements based on government approval'' and reported that ``My
colleagues in the Texas Senate are discussing an outright ban on ALPRs,
and I am right there with them'' (Lois Kolkhorst, n.d.). While Kolkhorst
was one of the most direct political elites to warn that Flock could
curtail individual liberty, concerns about Flock were bipartisan and
across the U.S., with Senators Ron Wyden (D) of Oregon, Adam Schiff (D)
of California, Josh Hawley (R) of Missouri, and Governor Ron DeSantis
(R) of Florida criticizing its current use or investigating its
safeguards in recent months (Office of Adam Schiff, n.d.; Talcott 2026;
Office of Josh Hawley 2026). Therefore, political elites took stances
against Flock in a similar window to the rise in unfavorable views of
Flock and increase in media coverage (See Figures 2 and 4).

\section{Three Pathways: Why Has This Fear Become More
Prominent?}\label{three-pathways-why-has-this-fear-become-more-prominent}

This section describes three explanations for why this fear has become
more prominent among some Americans. First is the media theory of fear,
which argues that the media may make surveillance seem more threatening
because it has an incentive to repeatedly report stories about
surveillance that can generate profit and be inexpensive to cover.
Second is the political elite theory of fear, which claims that
political elites may make surveillance appear more threatening when they
set the media agenda by investigating how law enforcement uses it,
issuing public statements about its risks, and proposing laws to
restrict it. Third is the political culture theory of fear, which posits
that U.S. political culture about the right to privacy as an individual
liberty may be influential in making surveillance seem more threatening.
\#\# The Media Theory of Fear

Media is one possible explanation for why the fear became more
prominent. As shown in Figure 4, the amount of news articles that
mentioned Flock increased in July and August 2026 and remained high
through September 27. Some articles within this sample have either
outright argued against Flock cameras or reported opposition to them in
headings such as ``Flock cameras are un-American. I'm glad folks are
fighting back'' or ``Texas privacy fight grows as Flock cameras spark
Fourth of July protest'' (Russell 2026; Humble 2026). Coverage of Flock
has also highlighted alleged misuse, such as a police officer making
more than 700 inquiries to allegedly stalk his estranged wife or a
police officer allegedly using more than 2,000 Flock searches to track
his ex-girlfriend (Russell 2026). Therefore, some media outlets have
presented Flock in ways that could invoke fear.

This coverage could increase fear of surveillance of ordinary people for
law enforcement. Romer et al.
(\textbf{romerTelevisionNewsCultivation2003?}) find that watching local
news was associated with a greater fear of crime after controlling for
crime rates. This finding aligns with the media theory of fear, in which
(1) people rely on and tend to trust news in terms of information and
(2) news organizations have incentives to cover subjects that
individuals might fear, since such coverage can be attention-grabbing
and generate profit while often being inexpensive to report
(\textbf{MediaFear?}). One can thus apply this model to the topic at
hand, such that repeated reports in the media of officers allegedly
misusing Flock may lead people who trust the news to fear that law
enforcement could misuse surveillance of ordinary people.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Hypothesis 1:} If Americans are exposed to sensational media
coverage of officers allegedly misusing Flock, then they will be more
likely to fear surveillance of ordinary people for law enforcement than
Americans who are not exposed to such coverage.
\end{quote}

\subsection{The Political Elite Theory of
Fear}\label{the-political-elite-theory-of-fear}

Another possible explanation for why the fear became more prominent is
the action of political elites. From the East Coast to the West Coast,
Democrats and Republicans have made statements, opened investigations,
and proposed policies aimed at controlling or removing Flock cameras
(Lois Kolkhorst, n.d.; Office of Adam Schiff, n.d.; Talcott 2026; Office
of Josh Hawley 2026). These negative positions about Flock have had
various justifications, ranging from fear of misuse by individual law
enforcement officers to warning that surveillance by the government
could limit individual liberty (Lois Kolkhorst, n.d.; Office of Josh
Hawley 2026). In so doing, politicians may have connected Flock cameras
to fears that law enforcement might misuse surveillance of ordinary
people or limit individual liberty.

This binding of the threat of misuse or a loss of liberty to Flock
cameras could increase fear of surveillance of ordinary people for law
enforcement. Kaufmann (\textbf{kaufmannThreatInflationFailure?}), in
describing how the Bush administration sold the Iraq war to the American
public, identifies that the administration inflated the threat that
Saddam Hussein posed by (1) setting the media agenda by focusing on the
threat Saddam Hussein posed to Americans in public speeches and
statements, (2) publicizing pieces of evidence that did favor their
views while withholding evidence that did not, and (3) leveraging the
White House's credibility on foreign policy (\textbf{PoliticiansFear?}).
When applied to this topic, U.S. politicians could make surveillance of
ordinary people for law enforcement seem more threatening if news
outlets report their statements and actions against Flock to Americans
who regard the politicians as credible.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Hypothesis 2:} If Americans are exposed to news coverage of
Flock that quotes politicians warning about its risks, then they will be
more likely to fear surveillance of ordinary people for law enforcement
than Americans who are exposed to similar coverage without politicians'
warnings.
\end{quote}

\subsection{The Political Culture Theory of
Fear}\label{the-political-culture-theory-of-fear}

Political culture is the last possible explanation for why the fear
might have become more prominent. In surveys from earlier decades,
Westin (2003) finds that Americans' concern about threats to personal
privacy was high, rising from 64 percent in 1978 to 84 percent in 1995,
in which 51 percent of Americans identified the government as the
greatest potential threat to privacy. More recently, about 70 percent of
Americans who responded to the World Values Survey Wave 7 in 2017 said
the government probably or definitely should not have a right to collect
information about people without their knowledge (EVS/WVS 2024).
Therefore, many Americans were concerned about government intrusions on
privacy both decades ago and in 2017, before the current debate about
Flock.

This ingrained belief in privacy could thus influence the attitudes of
some Americans toward Flock. The political culture theory of fear
suggests that politicians and news outlets may share and express fears
already held by a great number of the population
(\textbf{PoliticalCultureFear?}). For example, Gibson and Howard
(\textbf{gibsonRussianAntiSemitismScapegoating2007?}) found that
attitudes toward Jews changed little during the turmoil in Russia in the
1990s, even though some politicians blamed Jews for the country's
problems. On this basis, they argued that politicians' appeals depend on
beliefs the public already holds, since prejudice against Jews was not
widespread enough in Russia for those appeals to succeed. Using this
model, an existing concern about government intrusions on privacy may
make some Americans more likely to fear surveillance of ordinary people
for law enforcement when Flock receives more attention.
